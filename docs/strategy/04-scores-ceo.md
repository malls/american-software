## Candidate C1 — Micro-Credential Platforms for Mid-Market Upskilling (50–500 person firms)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **3**

The buyer is real and the category spend is proven, but the chain breaks exactly at our price point. Who pays is well-evidenced: all four incumbents have active mid-market customers at documented prices — LinkedIn Learning at $379.88/user/yr team tier, Degreed at $180/user/yr SMB, Docebo at a ~$40k median contract (competitive-lanes.md §C1 Pricing & Packaging Evidence, Tiers 1–4), and the 3–5x pricing cliff for 50–200 person orgs is structural, not anecdotal (competitive-lanes.md §C1 Gap 1). What is missing is the last two links of the chain: willingness to pay at the proposed $5–15/user/mo tier is explicitly untested and market size for the segment is unknown (00-longlist.md §C1 Evidence Completeness, labeled gap), and no reachable channel to mid-market L&D buyers is evidenced — incumbents reach this segment through sales-led custom quotes (competitive-lanes.md §C1 Gap 3), which is a channel we would have to build, not one that exists for us. Demonstrated category payment at 2–4x our target price keeps this off the bottom; the untested target tier and unevidenced channel keep it squarely mid-scale. Three numbers that matter: $379.88/user/yr (what the underserved pay today), $5–15/user/mo (what we'd charge — untested), and zero (primary willingness-to-pay data points at that tier).

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **2**

Everything in the evidence says this is a considered, sales-led B2B purchase, not a swipe-a-card one. Incumbent contracting norms for the segment are multi-year commitments and custom quotes with unclear minimums (competitive-lanes.md §C1 Gap 3: Skillsoft and Docebo run 3–5 year contracts standard; Degreed and Docebo lack self-serve pricing below a headcount threshold), and Docebo's documented $10,000–$40,000 implementation cost for mid-market deployment (competitive-lanes.md §C1 Incumbent Tier 4) tells us buyers in this segment expect HRIS integration, SSO, and migration before value — which means evaluation cycles, not impulse purchases. A training platform also needs the buyer's trust with employee development outcomes, and it needs content or credential frameworks in place before a first dollar is credible. Even executed well, the first external dollar sits behind a build plus a mid-market sales cycle measured in months; scoring 2, not 1, because the pricing-cliff pain is acute enough (competitive-lanes.md §C1 Gap 1) that an early-adopter deal inside two or three quarters is plausible — but not inside the build quarter, which is what a higher score requires under rubric §3.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **4**

This is close to standard terms-of-service territory, with one notch off for handling employee data at scale. Issuing non-accredited micro-credentials and hosting training content is unregulated commercial software — the gate already established that incumbents operate as ordinary SaaS vendors with no license or certified status (03-gate-verdicts.md §C1 G3), and nothing in the pricing/packaging evidence shows any incumbent carrying a regulatory apparatus (competitive-lanes.md §C1 — the incumbents' cost structures are sales- and content-driven). The continuing burden is the ordinary B2B SaaS stack: customer MSAs, a privacy policy and DPAs covering employee names and training records (garden-variety PII, no special category), and honest framing of what a non-accredited credential is so we never drift toward implying accreditation we don't have. That last item is a wording discipline, not a compliance apparatus. No trigger: score is above the lawyer-review line (rubric §3, Criterion C5).

## Candidate C2 — Lightweight Code Quality Gates for Small Dev Teams (2–20 devs)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **4**

The strongest revenue chain in the competitive set, held off a 5 by one honest question: does the bottom of this segment pay at all? Every link except the last is evidenced. Who pays: small dev teams are documented paying customers of Snyk, GitHub Advanced Security, and GitLab at published prices (00-longlist.md §C2 Evidence Completeness, Tier 1 established). How much: $1,500–$3,600/yr entry points with the exact misfit documented — Snyk's 5-developer minimum, GitHub's unpredictable per-committer billing, GitLab's 2025 removal of its lower tier forcing small teams to Free or a ~50% pricier Premium (competitive-lanes.md §C2 Tiers 1–4 and Gap 1, Gap 2). Channel: this is the one candidate with a genuinely self-serve, product-led route to the buyer — developers buy tooling off published pricing pages without a sales motion, as the incumbents' own packaging proves. The deduction is the labeled gap: there is no primary data on what 2–5 person teams currently spend, and the possibility that the underserved bottom of the segment simply accepts free tiers and OSS is real and documented (00-longlist.md §C2 labeled gap; competitive-lanes.md §C2 Gap 3 — free tiers exist but with no team features or SLA). A 5 requires demonstrated willingness to pay from the niche itself, not from its adjacent larger cousins.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **4**

Fastest plausible first dollar in the set, discounted one point for the trust asymmetry of security tooling. The purchase mechanics are as short as B2B gets: incumbents sell this exact product category self-serve at $9–$30/dev/mo off public pricing pages (competitive-lanes.md §C2 Tiers 1, 2, 4), entry prices sit at credit-card levels ($125/mo Snyk entry), and the buyer is the user — no procurement chain, no implementation project, no multi-stakeholder evaluation documented anywhere in the segment evidence. Nothing in the evidence shows a structural delay between product availability and first payment. The discount is a commercial judgment the evidence supports indirectly: this product asks a team to grant repository access to its most sensitive asset, and the incumbents small teams would compare us against are established brands whose free tiers are the documented default alternative (competitive-lanes.md §C2 Gap 3). Displacing "free from a name you trust" with "paid from a company you've never heard of" adds trust-building friction to the first dollars even when the checkout flow is instant. Revenue within the build quarter is genuinely plausible here; that is a 4-to-5 profile, and the unknown-vendor trust gap is what holds it at 4.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **3**

Unregulated, but not light: the continuing burden concentrates in what we hold and what we claim. No regulator touches this category — incumbents operate as ordinary SaaS (03-gate-verdicts.md §C2 G3) — so the surface is contractual, and that contract stack is heavier than commodity SaaS in three continuing ways. One, customer source code and repository credentials flow through us, which means confidentiality terms, security representations, and breach-liability exposure that scale with every customer added, and which customers in this category will actually negotiate. Two, the product's output is security claims — a missed vulnerability invites "your scanner said we were clean" disputes, so liability caps and disclaimers must be maintained as living documents, not boilerplate. Three, a v1 built on open-source scanning engines (competitive-lanes.md §C2 Gap 3, the OSS baseline) carries permanent license-compliance discipline for hosted commercial use — an IP-exposure line item that never closes (flagged as a carried item at gate co-sign; the cost side belongs to the CTO's criteria, the ongoing IP exposure belongs here). All manageable with standard instruments, none of it a compliance apparatus; a solid middle score. No lawyer trigger.

## Candidate C3 — Construction Permit & Compliance Data Aggregation

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **2**

Category payment exists, but the chain from *our v1* to a paying customer has two unevidenced links, and gate constraint 5 makes one of them worse. The evidence for payment in the category is real: BuildFax sells reports at $5 each through partner channels, ConstructConnect charges $129–199/mo with active customers, and 1,000+ construction compliance officer postings at $61k–$172k salaries prove firms spend heavily on this pain (competitive-lanes.md §C3 Incumbent Tiers 1–2, Job Board Signal). But the buyer for our specific product is unevidenced: there is no data on whether small construction firms — the underserved segment we would serve — actually buy permit data tools at all versus doing manual research (00-longlist.md §C3 Evidence Completeness, labeled gap). And the product we are allowed to sell first is coverage-constrained: under constraint 5 (03-gate-verdicts.md, CTO co-sign constraints), v1 draws only on free, ToS-compatible portals across a target of 100–500 jurisdictions out of ~19,000 (competitive-lanes.md §C3 Data Fragmentation Evidence) — and a permit-data product's value to any given firm is binary on whether *its* jurisdictions are covered. Selling partial coverage to an unvalidated buyer is two conditional probabilities multiplied together. What keeps this off a 1: the compliance-officer salary signal is the single strongest willingness-to-pay-for-the-pain datum in the whole competitive set, and per-report pricing (BuildFax's $5 unit) shows a route to revenue that doesn't require national coverage. Priced honestly per constraint 5: 2.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **2**

The first dollar sits behind a coverage threshold that constraint 5 says must be reached at $0, and nobody has measured how long that takes. Unlike a feature product, a data product cannot be thin and still sellable: the documented product shape is 100–500 key jurisdictions (competitive-lanes.md §C3 Niche Definition), and until enough of a buyer's jurisdictions are covered and fresh, there is nothing to charge for. Constraint 5 (03-gate-verdicts.md) restricts v1 acquisition to free public portals whose terms permit automated collection — with portal accessibility explicitly treated as unevidenced until measured — so the calendar time from commitment to a sellable dataset is an unknown multiplied by several hundred bespoke sources. Then comes the sale itself: the buyers are construction compliance and operations teams reached today by incumbent sales motions, not self-serve (ConstructConnect's $3,000+ implementation and training norms, competitive-lanes.md §C3 Gap 3), and no evidence shows small firms seeking out and buying such a tool unprompted (00-longlist.md §C3 labeled gap). Two sequential unknowns — time-to-coverage, then time-to-buyer — put the first dollar well past the build quarter on any honest read. Not a 1, because the per-lookup/per-report model (BuildFax's $5 report, competitive-lanes.md §C3 Tier 1) permits charging for narrow geography early if the covered jurisdictions happen to match a real buyer.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **3**

Lawful at the gate, but the continuing burden scales with the thing that makes the product valuable: source count. The practice itself is established — BuildFax, ATTOM, and Gryd aggregate and resell municipal permit records openly with no licensure (03-gate-verdicts.md §C3 G3), and the underlying records are public records. The recurring burden is threefold. One, constraint 5 converts per-portal terms review into a permanent legal operation: every jurisdiction added means verifying that its portal's terms permit automated collection, and re-verifying as terms change — at the documented product shape of 100–500 jurisdictions (competitive-lanes.md §C3 Niche Definition) that is a standing compliance process, not a one-time check. Two, reliance liability: customers make permitting and compliance decisions on our data, and the evidence notes aggregators do not even publish update-frequency SLAs (competitive-lanes.md §C3 Gap 4) — accuracy disclaimers and liability caps must carry real weight in every contract because stale or wrong permit data has direct customer consequences. Three, essentially no privacy surface — permit records are public, no special-category data. Established legality and no regulator keep this at 3 rather than lower; the per-source legal treadmill keeps it from 4. No lawyer trigger at this score — but flagged for the record: if v1 scope ever expands beyond free ToS-compatible portals, this criterion re-scores, and a 2 would trigger review.

## Candidate C4 — Workflow Automation for Recurring Compliance & Audit (mid-market)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **2**

This is the candidate where the revenue chain is inferred rather than observed — the structural gap is documented, the demand for a product in that gap is not. The gap itself is real and well-priced: Zapier's Team tier at ~$1,242/yr lacks governance depth, and the RPA floor starts at $75k/yr (Blue Prism) or $420/mo per developer (UiPath), leaving the $1k–$5k/yr middle empty (competitive-lanes.md §C4 Gap 3, Tick-2 §C4 update: "No middle ground exists"). But every demand-side link is a labeled gap: the defining use case is unvalidated ("compliance automation may be too broad," competitive-lanes.md Anomaly 3), there is no evidence mid-market compliance teams actively seek Zapier alternatives versus custom scripts or paper (competitive-lanes.md Tick-2 §C4: "No evidence yet"), no primary data on how these teams currently handle workflows at all, and no direct job-board or complaint signal for the specific product (00-longlist.md §C4 Evidence Completeness — the compliance-officer hiring signal belongs to construction, not this niche). Under rubric §3, a revenue path built on "the incumbents left a price gap, so someone must be standing in it" without an identified buyer or validated use case is the "someone would surely pay" pattern — kept off a 1 only because paying customers demonstrably exist on both sides of the gap (Zapier/Make and RPA both have compliance-use-case customers, 00-longlist.md §C4 Tier 1), so the spend pool is real even if our claim on it is unproven.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **2**

Two clocks run in sequence here, and the evidence says both are slow: find the use case, then earn the trust. First, because the defining workflow is unvalidated (00-longlist.md §C4 labeled gap; competitive-lanes.md Anomaly 3 recommends narrowing to specific audit/data-validation workflows before anything else), commitment does not start a build — it starts a discovery cycle that the first dollar waits behind. Second, the buyer is a compliance team purchasing audit integrity: the product's entire pitch is "trust us with the evidence trail your auditor will examine," and the evidence base shows this segment's trust bar operationally — Zapier's governance gaps are the documented reason the niche exists (competitive-lanes.md §C4 Gap 1, Gap 4), and the gate verdict already anticipates customers demanding SOC 2-class assurances from us post-revenue (03-gate-verdicts.md §C4 G4). A compliance buyer adopting an unknown vendor's product for regulated workflows is a considered, referenced, probably piloted purchase. Mid-market pricing at the target $200–500/mo (competitive-lanes.md §C4 Niche Definition) is credit-card-sized, which is why this is a 2 and not a 1 — if a specific painful workflow is found, a pilot could convert without enterprise procurement — but the discovery-then-trust sequence puts the expected first dollar several quarters out.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **2** — **LAWYER-REVIEW TRIGGER (rubric §3)**

The product's differentiation *is* an assumed compliance obligation, and that puts continuing legal burden at the heavy end of this set. We would be selling audit trails, change control, and role-based governance to teams whose regulatory posture depends on them — meaning a missed run, a lost log, or an access-control defect is not a bug but a customer's audit failure with our name on it, and the contractual exposure (representations about log integrity, retention, availability) must be actively managed in every deal, forever. Second, the connector model routes customers' compliance data through us, and the segment evidence points straight at regulated categories: Zapier's explicit HIPAA prohibition is cited as a gap we would be differentiating against (competitive-lanes.md §C4 Gap 1) — the moment a healthcare customer's workflow touches PHI, gate constraint 6 (BAA-before-PHI on every component, 03-gate-verdicts.md CTO co-sign constraints) lands on us as an operating obligation, board-gated spend and all. Third, the trust apparatus the segment demands — SOC 2-class controls and evidence (03-gate-verdicts.md §C4 G4), DPAs, security addenda — is a permanent, growing compliance program, exactly what rubric §3 describes at the low end of this criterion ("heavy continuing compliance apparatus"). Not a 1: none of this is a regulator licensing *us*, it is all contractual and certification burden, and it can be phased (e.g., contractually excluding regulated data categories at v1). **Per rubric §3, this score triggers lawyer-agent review before Candidate C4 may reach finalist status. The lawyer hire is a future tick under the board's standing grant.**
