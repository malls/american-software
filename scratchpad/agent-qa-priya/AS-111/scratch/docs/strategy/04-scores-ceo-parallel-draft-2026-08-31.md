## Candidate D1 — Freelance Writers & Content Creators (invoice/contract automation)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **3**

The niche itself demonstrably pays for this category — that is the strength — but the evidence for why it would pay *us* is thinner than the category numbers suggest. Who pays and how much is directly evidenced: freelancers are paying customers of FreshBooks at $49–99/mo, 17hats at $60/mo flat, Bonsai at $25/mo, and Plutio at $19/mo, with 8+ vendors competing and 71% of freelancers reporting payment difficulty (demand-lanes.md §D1 Evidence Summary; 00-longlist.md §D1); the pain is quantified to the decimal — 85% experience late payments, 29% of invoices paid ≥1 day late, 61% of late payments attributed to invoice errors (demand-lanes.md §D1 Tier 2). The channel is genuinely self-serve: this category sells off public pricing pages with no sales motion. Two links weaken the chain. First, the claimed $15–30/mo gap tier is already bracketed by incumbents — Plutio at $19/mo and Bonsai at $25/mo sit inside it (competitive-lanes.md §D1 Competitive Positioning) — so the underserved-tier thesis is thinner than the cross-validation pattern implies, and the real question becomes displacement, not gap-filling. Second, the researched differentiator was demoted by the researchers themselves: usage-rights tracking is "likely a nice-to-have… rather than a core pain point driving standalone product demand" (demand-lanes.md §D1 Tick-2 Gap Closure), and the surviving differentiator — contract-terms-to-invoicing integration (competitive-lanes.md §D1 Gap 2) — is a documented feature gap with no evidence anyone buys on it. Proven category payment and a real channel keep this solidly mid-scale; an occupied price band and an unproven differentiator keep it off a 4.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **4**

The purchase mechanics are about as short as they get: the buyer is the user, the price is card-sized ($15–30/mo target against incumbents at $19–99/mo), the channel is a pricing page, and no procurement, implementation, or multi-stakeholder evaluation appears anywhere in the segment evidence (competitive-lanes.md §D1). Constraint 7 helps the clock rather than hurting it — client payments settle directly to the freelancer's own processor account, so onboarding carries no custody apparatus on our side (03-gate-verdicts.md constraint 7). Value proof is fast in absolute terms: late-payment reminders act on live invoices within days to weeks, and reducing late payment *is* the evidenced pain (29% of invoices late, demand-lanes.md §D1). What holds this at 4 rather than 5: the first dollars must displace a free default (Wave) and cheap entrenched competitors with an unknown brand in a noisy, 8-vendor self-serve market, and the reminder-driven value story realistically takes an invoice cycle or two to demonstrate — which puts the modal first dollar at the edge of the build quarter rather than comfortably inside it.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **4**

Close to standard terms-of-service territory, with two standing G3 boundaries that must be actively maintained rather than merely written down. One: contract templates remain self-serve documents, never legal advice (03-gate-verdicts.md §D1 G3) — a permanent unauthorized-practice-of-law discipline covering template content provenance, review, and disclaimer language kept as living documents. Two: constraint 7's flow-of-funds boundary — we are never a payee and never in the flow of funds; client payments settle directly to the freelancer's own processor account — which is a design-review obligation on every payments-adjacent feature, forever, because the compliant architecture and the money-transmission architecture differ by one lazy shortcut. Beyond those, the surface is ordinary: garden-variety PII plus invoice metadata (no special-category data), consumer-grade ToS and privacy policy, processor platform terms to comply with. Incumbents (Bonsai, 17hats, FreshBooks) carry the identical posture as unregulated SaaS (03-gate-verdicts.md §D1 G3). One notch off 5 for the two maintained boundaries. No lawyer trigger.

## Candidate D2 — Solo Therapists & Coaches (lightweight HIPAA practice management)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **4**

Who pays, how much, and how often are all evidenced from inside the niche, at prices above our target. Solo practitioners demonstrably pay SimplePractice $49–99/mo plus a $35/mo AI add-on and TherapyNotes $69/mo plus a $40/mo AI add-on (demand-lanes.md §D2 Tier 1), they absorbed a 17% price increase in Dec 2025 without documented flight (demand-lanes.md §D2 Tick-2 — pricing inelasticity), and the real-cost analysis (advertised $59/mo vs. actual spend far above it via hidden add-ons, demand-lanes.md §D2 Tick-2) proves both the budget and the resentment. Carepatron at $31–39/mo proves a low-price entry is commercially viable in this exact segment (competitive-lanes.md §D2). The differentiator is evidenced, not invented: AI note-taking without speaker separation for couples/group therapy is a documented, unaddressed complaint with real liability stakes (competitive-lanes.md §D2 Gap 3; Nadia's r/therapists hallucination evidence), and the channel is self-serve pricing-page purchase, as the incumbents' own packaging shows. What holds this off a 5 is that the same evidence that proves the budget also proves the moat: switching costs are HIGH by the researchers' own gap closure — hidden-fee lock-in, compliance-driven switching friction, migration effort (demand-lanes.md §D2 Tick-2 Gap Closure) — and there is no primary data on switching rates or on how many solos are actively shopping rather than grumbling (00-longlist.md §D2 gap). The buyers exist and pay; their documented inertia is the unpriced risk in reaching them.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **2**

Two clocks run before the first dollar here, and both are documented. The first is compliance-before-commerce: constraint 6 requires an executed BAA on every component — host, database, email, transcription, logging, backups — before the first byte of PHI, with the enabling spend a board-approval event that *precedes* PHI, not follows revenue (03-gate-verdicts.md constraint 6). There is no compliant thin-v1-to-test-the-market in this niche; the compliance floor is the launch floor. The second is trust: the buyers are documented as risk-averse on precisely our axis — therapists publicly flagging non-BAA AI tools as liability (demand-lanes.md §D2 Tier 2, the Otter.ai compliance and lawsuit evidence), with switching friction the researchers rated HIGH — and the differentiating feature (AI notes for multi-speaker sessions) carries a clinical-liability quality bar a practitioner will test before paying. Against that: the purchase itself is card-sized and self-serve at $31–99/mo, and Carepatron's traction proves solos do move for a cheaper compliant option — which is what keeps this off a 1. On any honest read, compliance-first build plus trust-building against documented inertia puts the expected first external dollar quarters past the build quarter.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **2** — **LAWYER-REVIEW TRIGGER (rubric §3)**

Operating as a HIPAA business associate is a permanent regulatory apparatus, not a contract stack. The continuing burden: Security Rule compliance as a standing operational obligation; BAAs maintained upstream with every component vendor (constraint 6) and downstream with every customer; breach-notification duties under HHS rules plus state law overlays; and the exposure asymmetry that we would hold therapy notes — among the most sensitive PHI there is — where a breach is unrecoverable harm to the practitioner's clients, third parties who never chose us. Every feature addition re-opens the compliance analysis (a new integration is a new BAA question), and the AI-notes differentiator adds an accuracy/liability dimension on top of privacy. Not a 1, for reasons the evidence supports: this is a single, standardized, well-trodden regime — small vendors demonstrably carry it (Carepatron at $31–39/mo, competitive-lanes.md §D2; 03-gate-verdicts.md §D2 G3), the BAA chain is attainable self-serve on major hosts (constraint 6 verification), and the instruments are standard rather than negotiated per-deal. But rubric §3's "heavy continuing compliance apparatus" describes this candidate's steady state accurately. **Per rubric §3, this score triggers lawyer-agent review before Candidate D2 may reach finalist status — anticipated at gate co-sign (03-gate-verdicts.md §D2 verdict note).**

## Candidate D3 — Independent Musicians & Sound Creators (multi-DSP royalty aggregation & splits)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **2**

The gap is verified real; the buyer at the price is not. The tick-3 joint reconciliation — the most rigorously cross-examined finding in the evidence base — confirms no consumer-friendly, solo-artist-accessible unified royalty dashboard exists at the $10–50/mo price point (00-longlist.md §Critical Tick-3 Reconciliation). But nothing in the record evidences solo artists paying for standalone royalty *tracking* at any price: the Tier-1 spend is distribution (DistroKid $24.99/yr, CD Baby $9.99–14.99/release, artists multi-subscribing to cover DSP gaps, demand-lanes.md §D3), which is payment for a different job — and its price anchor is consumer-annual, an order of magnitude below the target band charged monthly. The researchers' own anomaly log flags exactly this question and it was never closed: whether solo musicians actively seek paid aftermarket tracking or accept the manual/calculator status quo (competitive-lanes.md Anomaly 1 — "Nadia to validate"; Tools 4 Music, the free calculator, is the documented alternative). "No product exists at this price point" is evidence of an unserved market or of no market, and this record cannot distinguish the two. Three things keep it off a 1: documented multi-subscription behavior shows artists pay redundantly to close feature gaps; the black-box royalty pool ($424M original 2021 MLC transfer, ~$160M pre-2021 remaining, corrected figures per the reconciliation) gives the product a self-funding pitch — a tool that finds money you are owed; and Curve Lite at £20/mo shows adjacent-segment (small label) willingness to pay a comparable price. Note for the model: constraint 7 forecloses any take-rate on money movement — splits are accounting plus initiation from the artist's own processor-managed account — so subscription is the entire revenue model, resting on exactly the unvalidated willingness above.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **2**

The value proof runs on royalty reporting cycles, and those cycles are slow. This product's one job, commercially, is showing an artist a dashboard that reconciles with their own statements — and an artist rationally pays after seeing it match at least one reporting cycle, where DSP and distributor reporting runs monthly-to-quarterly on lagged, non-aligned schedules (demand-lanes.md §D3: "different reporting formats and payment schedules"). Before that proof can even start, enough of a given artist's sources must be ingested to be useful, against per-source access methods the gate record treats as unevidenced (03-gate-verdicts.md §D3 G4 — artists' own exports/authorized data). So the sequence is coverage, then a months-long proof window, then payment. The mechanics that keep this off a 1: the purchase is consumer self-serve at card price, artist communities are cheap reachable channels (demand-lanes.md §D3 — r/WeAreTheMusicMakers et al.), and a free-ingestion-then-paid-reconciliation motion could convert early adopters whose statements match on the first cycle. But the expected first dollar sits past the build quarter, behind a proof window we do not control the length of.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **3**

Lawful and unregulated at the core — read-only aggregation of the artist's own data plus split accounting (03-gate-verdicts.md §D3 G3) — with three continuing lines that keep it from scoring higher. One: per-source data-access terms. The gate verdict explicitly forwards scraping-vs-authorized-export as a C5 burden question; every DSP or distributor source added means reviewing that source's terms, and re-reviewing as they change — a standing terms-compliance process, though at tens of sources rather than Candidate C3's hundreds. Two: money-grade reliance liability. Artists will claim royalties and pay collaborators based on our computations; an error has direct dollar consequences to identifiable third parties, so accuracy disclaimers, reconciliation caveats, and liability caps must carry real weight in every contract, permanently. Three: constraint-7 boundary maintenance — split *initiation* from the artist's own processor-managed account is the outer limit; any design drift toward routing third-party money through an account we control is unlicensed money transmission and the feature doesn't ship (03-gate-verdicts.md constraint 7). That boundary is a permanent design-review obligation with legal stakes, not a one-time architecture choice. Privacy surface is ordinary (financial data, standard PII). No regulator licensing us, standard instruments throughout: a solid 3. No lawyer trigger.

## Candidate D4 — Small Event Organizers (RSVP + no-show reduction)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **4**

Who pays, how often, and how they convert are all directly evidenced, with the strongest conversion datum anywhere in this candidate's record: 73% of organizers who start on a free event tool upgrade to paid within 90 days (demand-lanes.md §D4 Tick-2, Tier 1). Organizers pay Meetup $16–55/mo and have absorbed sustained price increases (demand-lanes.md §D4 — inelastic demand), and the target band is documented as structurally unserved: Meetup lacks no-show automation entirely, Eventbrite's $69/mo Premium is priced for 200+ attendee events, and Splash starts at $25k/yr — no sweet-spot solution exists at $30–100/mo (competitive-lanes.md §D4 Gap 1). The channel is self-serve, and the active "Meetup alternatives" comparison-content ecosystem (demand-lanes.md §D4 Tick-2 sources) is direct evidence organizers shop. Three things hold it off a 5. Willingness to pay for the integrated automation specifically is untested (00-longlist.md §D4 gap). The anomaly log flags a fork in the niche itself — no-show reduction and vendor coordination may be two products with different buyers and price points (competitive-lanes.md Anomaly 4), which is a scoping risk sitting directly on the revenue chain. And the free-event paradox: the organizers with the worst documented no-show pain (30–50%, free events) are precisely the ones with no event revenue to fund tooling, so the paying segment may be narrower than the pain statistics imply.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **4**

No documented friction in the purchase mechanics, and unusually fast value proof. The buyer is the user, the price is card-sized in the $30–100/mo band, there is no procurement or implementation step anywhere in the segment evidence, and the category's own behavior — 73% free-to-paid conversion inside 90 days (demand-lanes.md §D4 Tick-2) — shows organizers move quickly once a feature gap bites. The value claim is provable within one or two event cycles, measured in weeks: reminder automation reducing no-shows from ~50% toward 10–15% (demand-lanes.md §D4 Commentary) is visible at the very next event, and no-shows are the organizer's top documented frustration (the "flakers" complaint signal). Held at 4 rather than 5 on three frictions: the proof still requires those event cycles to run; organizers' communities live on Meetup, and the evidenced tolerance of Meetup's price hikes suggests network lock-in that slows displacement even when the tool is better; and messaging consent must be gathered before the first reminder legally sends, adding lead time to the first demonstration of value. Revenue within the build quarter is genuinely plausible; the modal first dollar lands just past its edge.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **4**

Standard terms-of-service territory plus exactly one live compliance line — and that line runs through the core loop. Reminders are the product, so telemarketing/messaging law (TCPA-class consent capture, opt-out honoring, sender registration, quiet hours) is a permanent operating obligation, not a launch checkbox: statutory per-message damages and an active plaintiffs' bar make sloppy consent plumbing an existential-sized exposure for a small company, and every message the product sends is inside that regime. The gate verdict correctly classified this as a continuing legal-surface cost rather than a licensure bar (03-gate-verdicts.md §D4 G3), and it is well-standardized: consent-by-design is a solved pattern, and incumbents operate the same rails at scale. Everything else is light — attendee names and contact details (ordinary PII, no special category), consumer-grade ToS and privacy policy, no regulator, no negotiated instruments. One notch off 5 for the messaging-compliance line being both permanent and core-loop. No lawyer trigger.

## Candidate D5 — Research Teams, Academic/Nonprofit (secure data collaboration)

### Criterion C1 (Revenue path): **2**

This is the candidate where the evidence explicitly declines to support the revenue chain at our segment. The labeled gap is unusually blunt for this evidence base: direct Tier-1 evidence for small-team (<$50K budget) secure-collaboration spending "DOES NOT EXIST in public sources" (demand-lanes.md §D5 Gap Remains; 00-longlist.md §D5). The buyer is unidentified — a PI with card authority, a department, or institutional procurement are commercially different animals and the record cannot say which we would sell to; the price point is untested; the channel is unevidenced; and uniquely on the long-list, board-network validation access is TBD rather than available (demand-lanes.md §D5 Board Network Access). What keeps it off a 1 is real adjacent payment on both sides of the segment: large initiatives pay for specialized platforms (Vivli, the Seven Bridges pay-your-own-cloud model), individuals pay $8/mo (Sync.com), Box's $20/user/mo shows small-team-shaped pricing exists, and grant-funded projects demonstrably absorb the 20–40% regulated-hosting premium (demand-lanes.md §D5 Tick-2) — so budget lines for secure data handling exist somewhere in the ecosystem. But the one adoption statistic we have cuts against our segment, not for it: 34% adoption below $1M organizational budgets versus 66% above (demand-lanes.md §D5 Tick-2) reads as the small end being priced out and staying out, not queueing for a cheaper option. As the chain stands, this is rubric §3's "someone would surely pay for this" pattern with better footnotes.

### Criterion C4 (Time to first dollar): **1**

Every clock documented in this niche is slow, and they run in sequence. Before any regulated dataset can land: constraint 6's BAA-before-PHI apparatus, with board-gated enabling spend preceding the first byte (03-gate-verdicts.md constraint 6). Before any institution's data arrives: the very governance friction that constitutes the pain — DUAs, IRB approvals, per-institution permission and audit-trail alignment (competitive-lanes.md §D5 Gap 2) — operates identically as a sales barrier, because a research team adopting a new custodian for irreplaceable primary data triggers institutional security review, not a card swipe. Before either: the buyer must be found at all, which Criterion C1 records as unvalidated, with no board intros to shortcut discovery. Academic and nonprofit money moves on grant cycles, and the documented low-cost workaround — ad-hoc Google Drive plus manual access control (demand-lanes.md §D5) — is free, which forecloses the one fast path (a single lab card-swiping for a non-regulated pilot) by putting us head-to-head with $0 for exactly the non-regulated use cases. Multi-institutional trust-building measured in quarters to years, behind compliance spend, behind buyer discovery: this is the rubric's long trust-building cycle, and it scores as one.

### Criterion C5 (Legal surface): **1** — **LAWYER-REVIEW TRIGGER (rubric §3)**

The definitional heavy end of this criterion: not one compliance regime but a stack of them, plus negotiated instruments that grow with every customer. The operating posture requires the full HIPAA business-associate apparatus (constraint 6 — everything Candidate D2 carries) *and* FERPA alignment *and* conformance with institutional DUA/IRB governance frameworks, with the segment evidence also referencing controlled-unclassified-information-grade expectations in research security (demand-lanes.md §D5 Tier 2, the EDUCAUSE signal). Unlike a single standardized regime, multi-institutional collaboration — the product's entire premise — means each participating institution brings its own security review, its own data-use agreement terms, and its own audit expectations: a contracting and compliance burden that is negotiated, cumulative, and never standardizes across the customer base. On top sits custody exposure of a kind no other legal instrument fully caps: we would hold researchers' irreplaceable primary datasets, where loss or breach is unrecoverable harm, so security representations, retention terms, and liability provisions carry maximum weight in every agreement, forever. That incumbents hold this posture as ordinary vendors (Box, Dataverse — 03-gate-verdicts.md §D5 G3) establishes possibility at the gate; Criterion C5 measures continuous cost, and here the steady state is a permanent, growing, multi-regime compliance apparatus — rubric §3's definition of a 1. **Per rubric §3, this score triggers lawyer-agent review before Candidate D5 may reach finalist status — anticipated at gate co-sign (03-gate-verdicts.md §D5 verdict note).**
